Contemporary Academic Persian: Syntactic Architectures, Register Taxonomies, and Editorial Standardization in Research Prose
Contemporary academic Persian, as codified in post-2018 peer-reviewed journals, doctoral dissertations, and institutional style manuals across Iranian universities, constitutes a highly formalized, distinct linguistic register [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02]. The evolution of this register is shaped by an ongoing structural tension between traditional administrative style rules, the terminological standardization protocols of the Academy of Persian Language and Literature (Farhangestan), and the emerging stylistic distortions introduced by neural machine translation engines and artificial intelligence drafting tools [cite: SRC-A02, 24, 38, 80].

Syntactic Principles and Clause Subordination
High-grade academic Persian demonstrates a structural preference for verb-final clause architectures characterized by complex multi-clause subordination anchored by non-finite verb forms, gerunds, or prefixed prepositional phrases [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A03, SRC-A06]. Rather than constructing series of short, independent coordinate clauses, expert academic prose embeds qualifying parameters, conditional limits, and methodological contexts prior to the primary predicate [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A06].

Prepositional Anchoring and Clause Order
In formal research reporting, contextual conditions and methodological parameters precede main predicate actions [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A03]. Prepositional phrases such as ba tavajjoh be (با توجه به - with regard to), bar asas-e (بر اساس - based on), ba estenad be (با استناد به - citing/relying upon), and jahat-e neil be (جهت نیل به - in order to achieve) function as syntactic anchors [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A06]. Placing these anchors at the clause opening establishes the scope of an empirical claim before the main predicate is introduced [cite: SRC-A01].

Syntactic cohesion requires controlling the distance between the primary subject and its governing verb [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02]. When introducing multiple independent or dependent variables, separating a subject from its predicate by more than three subordinate clauses disrupts comprehension [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02]. Senior academic editors address this by deploying summary introductory clauses or consolidating lengthy variable arrays into nominalized noun phrases before concluding the sentence [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02].

Passive Voice, Agency, and Authorial Stance
Academic Persian systematically minimizes direct authorial agency, enforcing an objective distance between the researcher and the empirical findings [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02, 4]. The first-person singular pronoun and its corresponding verbal inflections (e.g., anjam dadam - انجام دادم) are strictly excluded from formal thesis manuscripts and peer-reviewed journal papers [cite: SRC-A02, 4].

Instead, the register relies on two main structural alternatives [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A02]:

Third-person passive constructions utilizing compound passive verbs such as mored-e barresi gharar gereft (مورد بررسی قرار گرفت - was placed under investigation), sanjideh shod (سنجیده شد - was measured), or estekhraj gardid (استخراج گردید - was extracted) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04].

Implicit active third-person constructs assigning agency to the study or the data itself, using phrases such as pazhohesh-e hazir neshan midahad (پژوهش حاضر نشان می‌دهد - the present research indicates) or yafteha delalat bar an darad ke (یافته‌ها دلالت بر آن دارد که - the findings suggest that) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A03].

While first-person plural active verbs (e.g., barresi kardim - بررسی کردیم) have gained limited acceptance in qualitative auto-ethnographies to reflect researcher reflexivity, institutional thesis manuals—such as those from University of Tehran and Tarbiat Modares University—mandate passive constructions across quantitative methodology chapters and abstracts [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A06, 4, 11].

Epistemic Hedging and Claim Limitation
Academic Persian distinguishes empirical observations from theoretical inferences through precise hedging markers [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04, SRC-A06]. Absolute assertions of unmediated proof (e.g., in natijeh sabat mikonad - این نتیجه ثابت می‌کند) are systematically avoided in Discussion and Conclusion sections [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04].

Uncertainty, boundary limitations, and probabilistic outcomes are signaled using modal adverbs combined with indirect declarative verbs [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A06]:

Modal Adverbs: ehtemalan (احتمالاً - probably), be nazar mirasad (به نظر می‌رسد - it appears that), ta haddi (تا حدودی - to some extent) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04].

Indirect Verbs: delalat bar an darad ke (دلالت بر آن دارد که - points toward the fact that), mo'ayyed-e an ast ke (مؤید آن است که - supports the notion that), yafteha hamsoo ba... ast (یافته‌ها همسو با... است - the findings are aligned with...) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A06].

These structures maintain scientific modesty while insulating analytical claims against overgeneralization [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04].

Lexical Standardization, Terminology Protocols, and Collocations
The Persian academic lexicon is regulated through a dual framework: formal term-coining by the Academy of Persian Language and Literature (Farhangestan) and domain-specific collocations established across indexed publications [cite: SRC-A01, 24, 25, 31].

Terminology Standardization and Academy Protocols
The Academy of Persian Language and Literature applies a 13-stage protocol for evaluating foreign terms and establishing native equivalents. This procedure involves collecting specialized corpora, assessing morphological feasibility, evaluating usage in academic sub-disciplines, and securing institutional approval.   

In research methodology and data analysis, this process has established specific terminology standards [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A03, 49, 50, 52, 54]:

Foreign analytical concepts are assigned native Persian constructs, such as dadeh-kavi (داده‌کاوی) for data mining, or amareh (آماره) for statistical metrics.   

Methodological terms rely on specialized roots; for example, ravai (روایی) designates validity, while payai (پایایی) designates reliability [cite: SRC-A01, 52, 54].

When a foreign term lacks an established, widely accepted Farhangestan equivalent, standard practice dictates using the Persian transliteration within the body text and appending the original Latin term in a footnote upon first mention [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A03].

Natural Academic Verbs Versus Bureaucratic Clutter
High-level academic Persian favors precise semantic verbs that directly convey analytical actions [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A04]. Conversely, lower-quality academic texts and automated translations often rely on redundant compound verbs or bureaucratic filler [cite: SRC-A01, 38, 80].

Preferred Academic Verbs: estekhraj shod (استخراج شد - was extracted), tabyin namayad (تبیین می‌نماید - explains/explicates), ehsa gardid (احصا گردید - was enumerated), mored-e sanjesh gharar gereft (مورد سنجش قرار گرفت - was measured), ahraz shod (احراز شد - was attained/verified) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A03, SRC-A04].

Bureaucratic/AI Clutter to Avoid: Overusing be amal amad (به عمل آمد - took place), repeating soorat pazireft (صورت پذیرفت - was effected), or adopting direct English structural translations like mitvanad be عنوان یک فاکتور عمل کند (can act as a factor) [cite: SRC-A01, 38, 80].

Institutional Style Manuals and Structural Manuscript Requirements
Academic manuscripts in Iran are governed by precise formatting and structural protocols issued by major research universities—including University of Tehran (UT), Sharif University of Technology (SUT), and Tarbiat Modares University (TMU)—as well as indexing databases such as SID, Magiran, and Ensani [cite: SRC-A02, 3, 4, 5, 11, 14, 79].

University Thesis and Dissertation Manuals
Institutional thesis manuals prescribe a standardized sequence of front matter, body chapters, and back matter:   

Front Matter: Title page (Persian), Scan of Signed Approval Sheet (Tasvib-nameh), Declaration of Originality (Esharde-nameh), Dedication/Acknowledgements (optional), Abstract in Persian (Chekideh), Table of Contents, List of Tables, List of Figures, and List of Symbols/Abbreviations.   

Abstract Constraints: University manuals dictate that Persian abstracts must not exceed 300 words and must conclude with 4 to 7 precise keywords (Kelid-vazheh-ha) separated by semicolons.   

Physical and Digital Production Guidelines: Physical binding standards mandate specific color-coding: Burgundy (Zareshi) leatherette covers for Master's theses and Dark Green (Sabz) covers for Doctoral dissertations, featuring gold-foil hot stamping (Zarkoob) on the cover and spine. Spine text must display the title, author's name, and graduation year. Digital submission requires a unified PDF reflecting post-defense corrections.   

Standard Journal Article Structure (IMRaD Framework)
Peer-reviewed Persian journals strictly adhere to the standard IMRaD sequence:   

Title (Onvan): Concise, descriptive, devoid of unapproved abbreviations.   

Abstract & Keywords (Chekideh va Kelid-vazheh-ha): Context, objective, methodology, key findings, and main conclusion.   

Introduction & Literature Review (Moghadameh va Pishineh): Problem formulation, theoretical background, identification of literature gaps (shegaf-e pazhoheshi), and core research questions or hypotheses.   

Methodology (Rovesh-shenasi-ye Pazhohesh): Target population, sampling strategy, instrumentation, validation methods, and data collection procedures.   

Findings (Yafteha): Systematic presentation of empirical data, statistical tests, or qualitative coding matrices, supported by titled tables and figures.   

Discussion & Conclusion (Bahs va Natijeh-giri): Critical evaluation of findings against prior literature, contextual implications, research limitations, and actionable recommendations.   

Back Matter: Footnotes/Endnotes, Declarations of Competing Interest, Acknowledgments, and References (Fehrest-e Manabe) ordered alphabetically by author surname.   

Anti-AI Patterns, Translationese, and Syntactic Calquing
The proliferation of neural machine translation engines and large language models has introduced significant stylistic distortions into modern academic Persian. These distortions primarily manifest as syntactic and lexical calquing (Garteh-bardari - گرته‌برداری), where non-Persian linguistic structures are translated literal word-for-word, degrading sentence quality.   

Types of Calquing in Academic Texts
Linguistic analysis identifies three main categories of calquing that undermine formal academic prose:   

Lexical Calquing (Garteh-bardari-ye Vazagani): Substituting individual English words within idiomatic phrases literally. Examples include translating "to suffer from a disease" as ranj bordan az bimari (رنج بردن از بیماری) instead of the natural Persian mobtala boodan be bimari (مبتلا بودن به بیماری), or rendering "to count on someone" as hesab kardan rooy-e kasi (حساب کردن روی کسی).   

Syntactic Calquing (Garteh-bardari-ye Nahvi): Replicating foreign sentence structures. A frequent manifestation is introducing the passive agent using the preposition tavassot-e (توسط - by), directly echoing English passive structures. For example, writing dadeha tavassot-e narm-afzar SPSS tahlil shodan (داده‌ها توسط نرم‌افزار SPSS تحلیل شدند) violates native style [cite: SRC-A01, 80]. Natural academic Persian eliminates tavassot-e in favor of instrumental prepositional phrasing: tahlil-e dadeh-ha ba estefadeh az narm-afzar-e SPSS anjam gereft (تحلیل داده‌ها با استفاده از نرم‌افزار SPSS انجام گرفت) [cite: SRC-A01].   

Semantic Calquing (Garteh-bardari-ye Ma'nai): Expanding a native word's meaning to cover all senses of a foreign polysemous counterpart. For example, overusing touse'eh (توسعه) to translate every nuance of "development," or applying mo'arrefi (معرفی) whenever "introduce" appears in English, ignoring native alternatives like atfeh, ara'eh, or tashrih.   

AI Draft Fluff and Direct Translation Tropes
Automated text generation frequently inserts vacant introductory phrases and awkward translations of English idioms [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05, 38, 80]. High-grade editorial protocols explicitly flag and remove these patterns [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05]:

Vacuous Introductory Fluff: Phrases like dar jahan-e emrooz, ahammiyat-e... bar kasi pooshideh nist (در جهان امروز، اهمیت... بر کسی پوشیده نیست - In today's world, the importance of... is clear to everyone) lack scholarly substance [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05]. Academic introductions open directly with the research problem or conceptual framework [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05].

Direct Translation of Idiomatic Metaphors: Literal translations such as naghsh-e X ra bazi mikanad (نقش X را بازی می‌کند - plays the role of X) or dar payan-e rooz (در پایان روز - at the end of the day) sound unnatural in Persian prose. They are replaced with natural alternatives like naghsh-e X ra be ohdeh darad (نقش X را به عهده دارد) and dar jam-bandi-ye nehai (در جمع‌بندی نهایی) [cite: SRC-A01, SRC-A06].   

Redundant Prepositional Stacking: Combining multiple causal prepositions, such as be manzoor-e jahat-e be dast avardan-e (به منظور جهت به دست آوردن), creates structural bloat [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05]. Modern style guidelines mandate concise prepositional phrases such as baraye dast-yabi be (برای دست‌یابی به) or jahat-e neil be (جهت نیل به) [cite: SRC-A02, SRC-A05].

Systemic Disagreements and Methodological Ambiguities
